Parks as a third space in Bangalore

Who gets to hangout?

Parks—spaces where, by the virtue of its nature, we immediately understand that it’s a place where everyone is allowed. As soon as we put “Parks” and “Bangalore” in the same sentence, a bunch of names come up—Cubbon Park, Lal Bagh, Freedom Park—to name a few of the lot. For a space we think of as something open for all, we do notice diversity amongst the population, which varies from park to park. This sets us up for a question: Who gets to hang out?

A brief history · before the 1800s

Gardens before parks

Long before Bangalore had official parks, the land was covered in kitchen gardens planted for food. In the 1700s, Hyder Ali and his son Tipu Sultan laid out the city’s first landscaped gardens.

A brief history · after 1799

A place to spend time

After the British defeated Tipu Sultan and took over the city, Lalbagh became a horticultural hub, and an annual flower show soon followed. British officials and local residents came to see it. Green space was no longer only for growing food; people went there to spend time.

A brief history · today

More parks, less green

The IT boom brought more people and denser building. Green cover fell even as the number of parks grew. The BBMP now lists over 1,200 neighbourhood parks covering about 2 sq km, most with concrete walks, play areas and exercise zones for senior citizens.

Third spaces

Hanging out requires you to occupy spaces. A truly enjoyable hangout, without any restrictions, happens in a space as open as the people. These spaces, as coined by sociologist Ray Oldenburg in his 1989 book The Great Good Place, are known as third spaces—a social environment separate from your home (the first place) and your workplace or school (the second place).

Third spaces

These third spaces have been important places to hang out freely. Cafés, libraries, and pubs are great examples of these spaces. It gives people the freedom to exist without judgement. The everyday hangouts become easier with these spaces.

Third spaces

Parks also serve as one of these third spaces. An open space to sit, talk, wind down, and be as you are. What sets it apart from other third spaces is that there are no limits to it. When it claims to be "open" it truly is. Cafés can be exclusive to those who have a certain income. Libraries are exclusive to the literate. Pubs, again, are exclusive in terms of age and social standing.

Third spaces

Parks, however, have none of that.

The coffee house and the ashram

In "The Coffee House and the Ashram: Gandhi, Civil Society and Public Spheres," by Susanne and Lloyd Rudolph, the authors compare the European concept of the “public sphere” to public life during Gandhi’s time in India. They contend that Gandhi’s ashram expanded the notion by incorporating daily life, spirituality, and common people into the public arena. While the European coffeehouses were highly exclusive, open to only those who were literate, Gandhi's Ashrams did not have such caveats.

The coffee house and the ashram

Similarly, parks do not have any conditions attached to them. Just like Gandhi's Ashrams, they stay open to all individuals, creating a space where everyday life becomes a general happening within these spaces.

The coffee house and the ashram

Besides the obvious, parks can also serve as political spaces—locations of resistance. Recent movements and gatherings in Freedom Park prove this. It is similar to how Gandhi used his Ashrams. "Ashrams are retreats for those who wish to join a community of dedication...in Gandhi’s case also to wider political interests and to social service—projects Gandhi regarded as paired," the authors quote in the essay.

What we found

So, who gets to hang out? The question is, who doesn't?

What we found

Parks in Bengaluru have been social spaces for a while. If anyone wants to enter, they do so with no qualms. In all the parks we visited, not one of them felt closed off or like we weren't allowed; even the residential parks were inviting. The range of people we saw, from young kids to old groups, from a lone jogger to friends catching up, dog walkers to strays that found their way in, proves that the park gates never close. They stay open and welcoming.

What we found

The lack of restrictions on who is "allowed" is one of the major reasons why parks are so loved. Accessibility is another. Parks give solitude to those who need it and community to others. It is an open space, ready to be moulded into whatever you need it to be.

What we found

So the question isn't "Who gets to hang out" but rather "How do they hang out?" And for that, the possibilities are endless.
